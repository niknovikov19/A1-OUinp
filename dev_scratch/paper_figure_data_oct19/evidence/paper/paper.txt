Title
Circuit decoupling method for baseline regime tuning in a biophysically detailed thalamocortical
model

Introduction
Biophysically detailed circuit models integrate data on neuronal properties, synaptic interactions,
and circuit anatomy to support in silico experiments (Arkhipov et al., 2018; Billeh et al., 2020;
Dura-Bernal et al., 2023; Isbister et al., 2026). Direct measurements constrain some model
parameters, while others remain weakly constrained and need to be tuned so that simulated
activity matches physiological recordings. Such tuning often requires a high-dimensional search,
with candidate parameter combinations evaluated through computationally expensive
simulations. Despite these challenges, several optimization methods have been successfully
applied to biophysical model tuning (Dura-Bernal et al., 2019, 2023), and new approaches
continue to emerge (Deistler et al., 2025). Nevertheless, reproducing physiological circuit
activity remains difficult and requires tuning procedures adapted to the model and available data
(Dura-Bernal et al., 2024; Tolley et al., 2024).

An important goal of model tuning is to establish a physiologically appropriate baseline regime
with realistic spontaneous activity. Experiments demonstrate that response properties depend on
ongoing activity in both individual cells (Chance et al., 2002; Fellous et al., 2003) and neuronal
populations (Petersen et al., 2003; Curto et al., 2009; Zagha et al., 2013). Nonlinear recurrent
network models likewise show that circuit dynamics vary with activity level even when synaptic
weights remain fixed (Ahmadian et al., 2013; Rubin et al., 2015; Hennequin et al., 2018).
Establishing an appropriate baseline is therefore necessary to reproduce circuit responses under
the physiological condition of interest.

There are two main tuning approaches used for setting the baseline activity. It can be done either
by tuning recurrent synaptic weights (Arkhipov et al., 2018; Billeh et al., 2020; Dura-Bernal et
al., 2023) or population-specific background inputs that represent aggregate synaptic activity
from the sources outside the modeled circuit (Jiang et al., 2024; Isbister et al., 2026). Weight
tuning can make the circuit more self-sufficient in generating baseline activity, but it is
computationally demanding and can inadvertently distort circuit responses to incoming stimuli.
By comparison, tuning background inputs while keeping recurrent weights fixed is more
straightforward, but it creates a dependence on external drive of unspecified biological origin.
Each approach has its own strengths and limitations, and the choice of tuning strategy depends
on the available constraints and modeling objectives.

Even with recurrent weights fixed, background-input tuning remains challenging because the
input to one population can affect the whole circuit. A direct approach would be to tune
population-specific inputs jointly, which requires a potentially costly search in a high-
dimensional parameter space. To simplify the search, one could remove the recurrent
connections and tune each population’s inputs independently. This requires correcting for any
mismatch in baseline activity that may arise when connectivity is restored. One such method was
proposed by Isbister et al. (2026). They measured how each population’s firing rate depended on
background input in isolation, and then iteratively estimated a mapping between population
firing rates in disconnected and connected circuits. This mapping allowed them to identify the
background inputs expected to produce the target rates once recurrent connectivity is restored.

===== PDF PAGE 2 =====
The method reached the target regime within a few iterations in their model, although the study
did not establish general conditions for convergence. This motivates exploring complementary
approaches to simplifying the high-dimensional search for background inputs.

Here we present a method for establishing the baseline regime in multi-population models via
background input tuning based on circuit decoupling with static surrogate compensation for the
omitted recurrent connections. The method allows each population’s background input to be
tuned independently under synaptic conditions approximating those expected in the recurrent
circuit operating in the target baseline regime. We apply the method to a modified version of our
previously published 43-population model of macaque auditory thalamocortical circuit (Dura-
Bernal et al., 2023) and assess whether background inputs tuned in the decoupled setup yield
near-target regime when recurrent connectivity is restored. We also explore how our method
could help identify and overcome issues that emerge along the tuning process.

Results

Circuit decoupling with surrogate compensation
We developed a method for tuning background inputs in multi-population models based on
circuit decoupling with automatic replacement of removed recurrent connections by
compensatory surrogate inputs. In a fully decoupled circuit, cells do not affect each other
through recurrent connections, so the background inputs can be tuned independently for each
population. This transforms a high-dimensional optimization problem into a set of independent,
low-dimensional problems that can be solved in parallel. We construct the surrogate inputs to
approximate ((unclear verb or adjective)) the synaptic inputs that cells would receive in the
original circuit if its populations fired at their target rates. Our rationale is that background inputs
tuned in a decoupled model with such surrogate compensation could provide candidate solutions
for the original model as well, bringing the original recurrently connected circuit close to the
target regime.

We illustrate the method using a hypothetical circuit containing two populations, P1 and P2 (Fig.
FIG_SURR_NET(A)). The circuit contains within- and cross-population recurrent projections
Pn→Pm (m,n = 1,2), each comprising a set Cmn of individual cell-to-cell connections. In
addition, each cell receives two background synaptic inputs, one excitatory and one inhibitory,
driven by independent Poisson spike generators. The background input rates and synaptic
weights are defined separately for each population and shared among its cells. We keep the
synaptic weights fixed and adjust the background rates to make P1 and P2 fire at their respective
target mean rates, r10 and r20.

A fully decoupled version of the circuit is shown in Fig. FIG_SURR_NET(B). To compensate
for the omitted recurrent inputs, we introduce a surrogate population Pn′ for each original
population Pn, consisting of independent Poisson spike generators that fire at the target rate rn0
assigned to Pn. We then replace every recurrent projection Pn→Pm with a corresponding
surrogate input Pn′→Pm. We connect the surrogate units of Pn′ to cells of Pm following the
same rules as for the original Pn→Pm projection, preserving connection probabilities and the
distributions of synaptic weights and transmission delays. We position the surrogate units
according to the same spatial distribution as the cells of Pn, thus preserving distance-dependent
terms in the connectivity rules. In the resulting circuit, the original cells and their background
inputs remain unchanged, while their recurrent inputs are replaced by inputs from surrogate
spike generators.

In the decoupled circuit, the surrogate inputs are independent of the activity of P1 and P2.
Changing the background input to either population changes its firing rate without affecting the
inputs received by the other population, allowing the background inputs to the two populations to
be tuned independently ((“populations” repeats too much)). If P1 and P2 were to fire at their
target rates in the original circuit, the corresponding recurrent and surrogate projections
((confusing; sounds like recurrent and surrogate exist within the same original circuit)) would
have equal mean presynaptic firing rates and follow the same connectivity rules (Fig.
FIG_SURR_NET(A,B)). This suggests that the background inputs tuned in the decoupled circuit
may also produce firing rates near the targets in the recurrent circuit. More generally, for a
circuit containing N populations, a joint search over 2N background-input parameters can be
((potentially)) replaced by N independent two-parameter searches. However, successful tuning in
the decoupled circuit does not guarantee that the recurrent circuit will reach the target regime, so
the resulting background inputs must be tested after recurrent connections are restored.

Our framework additionally allows partial decoupling, in which only selected recurrent
connections are replaced by surrogate inputs while the remaining connections are preserved (Fig.
FIG_SURR_NET(C)) ((link to the previous parag)). This provides two complementary ways to
examine circuit dynamics. First, we can examine the contribution of individual projections or
groups of projections by replacing them with surrogate inputs while leaving the rest of the
recurrent circuit intact. Second, we can isolate a selected subcircuit by retaining its internal
recurrent connections and replacing inputs from the rest of the circuit with surrogate inputs.
Figure FIG_SURR_NET(C) illustrates both operations for a three-population circuit.

Finally, we implemented semi-surrogate connections, which combine recurrent and surrogate
inputs within a single projection (Fig. FIG_SURR_NET(D)). For each connection with original
synaptic weight w, we introduce recurrent and surrogate components with weights kw and
(1−k)w, respectively, where 0 ≤ k ≤ 1 ((not for each connection – for each projection:
connectivity is sampled anew)). Thus, k = 0 corresponds to a fully surrogate projection, while k
= 1 corresponds to the original recurrent projection. By varying k gradually over time, we can
smoothly replace surrogate input with recurrent input without abruptly restoring the full
recurrent feedback ((implicit “input=feedback”)). This allows us to examine how the circuit
responds to the restoration of selected connections and whether the target regime can be
maintained as recurrent interactions are reinstated.

Background input tuning in a fully decoupled circuit
We applied our method to a modified version of our previously published biophysical model of
the macaque auditory thalamocortical circuit, containing 43 recurrently connected populations
(Dura-Bernal et al., 2023). Our objective was to establish a baseline regime in which each
population's mean firing rate matched a chosen target. We chose the target rates from published
experimental measurements and estimated missing values by extrapolating available evidence
across species, brain regions, layers, and cell types (Table TBL_TARGET_RATES). We denote
the vector of these 43 target rates by R0.

The version used here differed from the published model in its background-input configuration:
each neuron received excitatory and inhibitory Poisson inputs representing synaptic activity from
sources outside the modeled circuit. Neurons within a population shared the two input rates but

===== PDF PAGE 5 =====
received independently generated spike trains. We held both recurrent and background-input
synaptic weights fixed and tuned only the two Poisson rates for each population. Across the 43
populations, this gave 86 tunable background rates.

We first searched for background rates that would make the populations fire at their targets in a
fully decoupled version of the circuit. Each modeled population retained its background inputs,
while its recurrent inputs were replaced by inputs from surrogate populations. The spike
generators in each surrogate population fired at the target mean rate assigned to the
corresponding modeled population. Figure FIG_BKG_GRID(A) illustrates this configuration for
one population, P, with excitatory and inhibitory background rates rxpe and rxpi, respectively,
grouped as hp=(rxpe,rxpi). A1' represents the surrogate copy of the entire circuit that supplies
compensating inputs to P. Because the surrogate inputs were independent of the modeled
populations' activity, adjusting one population's background rates left the inputs to other
populations unchanged. The task of choosing 86 background rates could therefore be carried out
as 43 independent 2-D searches, each over the excitatory and inhibitory background rates of one
population.

We performed each of these searches in two stages: a coarse scan followed by a finer scan.
Figure FIG_BKG_GRID(B,C) shows the resulting firing-rate maps for the illustrated population
P. For the coarse scan, we simulated the fully decoupled circuit with background rates sampled
from a grid spanning a broad rectangular region of the (rxpe,rxpi) plane and measured the
population's mean firing rate for each combination. The coarse map showed a narrow,
approximately diagonal band of background rates for which the population fired near its target,
rp0 (Fig. FIG_BKG_GRID(B)). We defined a parallelogram Qp around a portion of this band
and performed a finer scan within it, using transformed coordinates in which Qp appeared as a
rectangle (Fig. FIG_BKG_GRID(C)). From the finer measurements, we identified a contour
along which the population's mean firing rate equaled its target. We selected the background-rate
pair at the midpoint of this contour within Qp as the solution and denote this pair by
hp*=(rxpe*,rxpi*).

The searches yielded a set of selected background rates for all 43 populations, which we denote
by H* (Table TBL_RX_WX). To test these rates across different model realizations, we ran 15
simulations of the fully decoupled circuit, holding H* fixed while resampling the connectivity
and Poisson spike trains. For the illustrated population P, the firing rate fluctuated around its
target after an initial transient (Fig. FIG_BKG_GRID(D)). Its mean firing rate remained near the
target across the 15 realizations (Fig. FIG_BKG_GRID(E)).

We calculated firing-rate errors as the differences between simulated mean population rates and
the corresponding target rates. Across the 15 realizations, most populations had error
distributions centered near zero [stats], although the distribution widths varied across populations
(blue box plots in Figs. FIG_CMP_SURR_FULL(C) and FIG_THAL(D); summary statistics in
Table TBL_ERR_STATS). We next asked whether the selected background inputs would also
produce firing rates near the targets when recurrent connections were restored.

Cortex
All-surrogate setup

We start our analysis from the cortical part of the thalamocortical model, while replacing all
thalamic populations with their surrogate copies.

First, we evaluate the quality of our background tuning using the all-surrogate setup, in which all
recurrent inputs are replaced by static surrogates (Fig. FIG_CMP_SURR_FULL(A)). We take
our tuned background input and run 15 simulation trials with different random seeds. The per-
cell random connectivity differs across trials, as do the realizations of the input Poisson spike
trains. For each trial, we compute the resulting firing rates {rpn_surr} (p – population, n – trial)
by averaging over the last 3 seconds of simulation and compare them to the target rates {rp0}. In
Fig. FIG_CMP_SURR_FULL(C), blue vertical bars represent the statistics of the error
{rpn_surr-rp0} across trials. One can see that the errors are mostly centered around zero, with
relatively low variance, as expected for a setup similar to the one used for background tuning.          Добавлено примечание ([НН1]): Numbers

Fully connected model

Next, we plug the background input into a full cortical model, where all intracortical connections
are active, while thalamocortical inputs remain surrogate (Fig. FIG_CMP_SURR_FULL(B)).
Again, we run 15 trials with different seeds, compute the average rates Rn_full={rpn_full} in
each trial, and compare them to the target R0={rp0}. The error distributions across trials are
shown in Fig. FIG_CMP_SURR_FULL(C) by orange bars. One can see that the resulting state
Rn_full strongly deviates from the target in most trials.

There are several things that could potentially happen with the near-target state Rn_surr
observed in the all-surrogate setup (its distance from R0 is shown by the blue bars). First, it could
disappear or lose stability, causing the system to converge to a different state Rn_full. Second, it
could persist but shift from Rn_surr to Rn_full. Third, it could remain stable and co-exist with
Rn_full, making the system bistable: initial conditions would then determine whether the system
converges to a near-target state close to Rn_surr or to a second state Rn_full located far from the
target.

Model with static self-excitation

At this point, we confirmed that the model has the correct state in the all-surrogate setup but
converges to a wrong state when all connections are recurrent. Next, we try to identify a set of
connections responsible for this discrepancy (not necessarily a minimal one). Loss of stability
and emergence of bistability are often associated with recurrent self-excitation. Thus, we
consider a version of the cortical model in which all excitatory-to-excitatory (E-E) connections
are surrogate (i.e. static), while the other connections remain recurrent
(FIG_CMP_EE_SURR_FADE(A)).

As before, we ran 15 simulation trials with different random seeds. The resulting rate
distributions are shown in FIG_CMP_EE_SURR_FADE(C) by green bars. One can see that the
simulated states remain close to the target (cf. the orange bars in Fig.
FIG_CMP_SURR_FULL(C)). This confirms that at least some subset of the recurrent E-E
connections is responsible for the deviation from the target observed in the full model.

Fading from static to recurrent self-excitation

The remaining question is whether the full model actually lacks a stationary near-target regime,
or whether such a regime exists but the simulations start from a point outside its basin of
attraction. To answer this question, we start from the model with surrogate E-E connections,
simulate it for 3 seconds to let the regime settle, and then smoothly fade out the surrogate E-E
connections while fading in the recurrent E-E connections (FIG_CMP_EE_SURR_FADE(B)).
The transition lasts for 2 seconds, after which the model becomes fully recurrent. We then
continue the simulation and compute the resulting state as for the other models.

The result is shown in FIG_CMP_EE_SURR_FADE(C) by red bars. One can see that the model
remains close to the initial near-target state even after the self-excitation becomes fully recurrent.
Note that the red bars in FIG_CMP_EE_SURR_FADE(C) and the orange bars in
FIG_CMP_SURR_FULL(C) correspond to the same fully connected model, which settles into
different regimes depending on the initial conditions.

Thalamus
All-surrogate and fully connected models

Having tuned the cortical model with surrogate thalamic inputs, we next turn to the
complementary case: tuning the thalamic model with surrogate cortical inputs (Fig.
FIG_THAL(A)). As before, we start by confirming the adequacy of the background tuning. To
do this, we simulate 15 all-surrogate versions of the model and compare the resulting firing rates
with the targets. Distributions of the firing-rate errors across 15 random trials are shown in Fig.
FIG_THAL(D) by blue bars, and one can see that the errors are close to zero.

Next, we run the full thalamic model with active recurrent connections (Fig. FIG_THAL(B)).
The resulting error distributions are shown in Fig. FIG_THAL(D) by orange bars and reveal
systematic deviations from the target for some populations. This prompted us to apply the same
approach as for the cortical model: freezing candidate subsets of connections and examining the

===== PDF PAGE 10 =====
resulting errors. We first tested E-E connections, but unlike in the cortex, freezing them did not
correct the discrepancy. We therefore tested other subsets and identified one whose freezing
corrected most of the systematic deviation from the target (see details in Supplementary).
However, smoothly fading these connections from surrogate to recurrent versions always led to
reappearance of the deviation. Thus, we conclude that the thalamic tuning error has a different
nature from the one observed in the cortex. The system is not bistable and does not converge to
an alternative regime that co-exists with the near-target one. Instead, the surrogate-based
background prediction itself is inaccurate: the background input that yields a near-target regime
in the all-surrogate setup produces a regime that deviates from the target when all recurrent
connections are active.

To identify the source of this discrepancy, we examined the thalamic activity more closely and
found that the firing-rate distributions across cells are very broad. This is an expected                 Добавлено примечание ([НН2]): Ref to supplementary
consequence of the small number of thalamic cells in the model combined with random
connectivity and non-fixed in-degree. Such broad distributions strongly violate the assumption of
the surrogate setup that all presynaptic units have approximately equal firing rates.

Second stage of background optimization

To reduce the error, we add a second tuning stage in which the background inputs to all thalamic
populations are jointly optimized, with the surrogate-based prediction used as the initial point.
Despite its inaccuracy, this prediction is relatively close to the target and therefore serves as a
better starting point for optimization than a random guess. Given the initial proximity to the
target and the relatively small number of thalamic populations, joint optimization is feasible.

As before, we generate 15 versions of the model with different random seeds. We then optimize
the background input separately for each version, starting from the same initial point. As a result,
we obtain 15 different background-input corrections, one for each model realization
(schematically shown in FIG_THAL(C)). The green bars in Fig. FIG_THAL(E) show the
distributions of these corrections across the 15 realizations, and the green bars in Fig.
FIG_THAL(D) show the residual distributions of the post-optimization errors. One can see that
both the systematic error and the variance are substantially reduced compared to the orange bars.

Our goal is to find a background input that performs well across different random realizations of
connectivity and Poisson spiking. Therefore, we collect the optimized background corrections
from all 15 models and calculate the median correction for each population (Fig. FIG_THAL(E),
red horizontal marks). The sum of the surrogate-based background prediction and this median
correction is used as the final solution for the thalamus. The resulting error distributions are
shown in Fig. FIG_THAL(D) by red bars. The variance predictably increases compared to the
green bars, but the systematic errors change only slightly, supporting the use of median
corrections instead of model-specific optimized values.

Thalamocortical circuit
Finally, we tested the tuned parameters in the full thalamocortical model. We used the
background inputs obtained in the surrogate open-loop model and re-applied the second-stage
corrections previously found for the isolated thalamic subcircuit (Fig. FIG_THAL(E)). As in the
isolated cortical subcircuit, the near-target regime was not reached from naïve initial conditions.
We therefore used the same surrogate-to-recurrent fading procedure. Initially, all excitatory-to-
excitatory connections were replaced by surrogate inputs (Fig. FIG_A1(B,C), left). After the
initial transients decayed, the surrogate inputs were gradually faded out while the corresponding
recurrent connections were restored between 8 and 10 s. The circuit remained near the target
regime after recurrent connectivity was fully restored (Fig. FIG_A1(B,C), right).

We evaluated the resulting firing rates across 15 random realizations of connectivity and
background activity using the same tuned parameters. Mean firing-rate errors remained small for
most populations, although the magnitude and variability of the errors differed across
populations (Fig. FIG_A1(A)). The largest residual deviations occurred in TC, VIP2, and VIP3.
These populations showed larger errors than in the corresponding isolated cortical or thalamic
subcircuits, consistent with an effect of thalamocortical and corticothalamic interactions that
were absent during the preceding subcircuit tuning stages. In the fully recurrent regime,

Discussion
In this work, we developed a circuit-decoupling method with surrogate compensation for
baseline firing-rate tuning in recurrent circuit models. We applied it to our 43-population
biophysically detailed model of the macaque auditory thalamocortical circuit. Using full
decoupling, we tuned the background inputs for each population separately and reached the
target firing rates in the open-loop setup. We then tested the open-loop solution separately in the
isolated cortical and thalamic subcircuits with compensated partial decoupling. Both subcircuits
deviated substantially from the target, but our tests suggested different causes. The cortical
subnetwork was able to sustain a near-target regime, but with naïve initialization it settled in a
coexisting regime far from the target. A gradual surrogate-to-recurrent cross-fade of E→E
connections solved the issue and gave access to the near-target regime. In the thalamic
subcircuit, we found no evidence for a similar accessibility problem, so the deviation from the

===== PDF PAGE 13 =====
target likely stemmed from limited accuracy of surrogate compensation. An additional
multidimensional tuning of the thalamic subcircuit yielded corrections to the open-loop solution
and reduced the errors. Finally, we combined the open-loop background solution, thalamic
corrections, and fading procedure in the full thalamocortical model. Across 15 random model
realizations, most population firing rates stayed near the target, although considerable errors
remained in several cases. Overall, compensated full decoupling enabled population-wise
background tuning, while partial decoupling helped diagnose and address the problems that
appeared when recurrent connectivity was restored.

Circuit decoupling for background-input tuning
Establishing an appropriate baseline regime in a neural circuit model is important for achieving
correct dynamics, including the responses to transient inputs. Impact of the baseline on dynamic
properties was experimentally shown both for individual cells (Chance et al., 2002; Fellous et al.,
2003) and neural circuits (Arieli et al., 1996; Petersen et al. 2003; Curto et al., 2009; Zagha et al.,
2013; McGinley et al., 2015; Reig et al., 2015; Gutnisky et al., 2017). Baseline activity shapes
the effective inter-cell coupling, so changing the baseline can alter circuit dynamics even when
synaptic weights remain fixed (Ahmadian et al., 2013; Rubin et al., 2015; Hennequin et al.,
2018).

A practical way to define the baseline state of a circuit model is to specify the firing rates of its
constituent populations. Methods for achieving these baseline rates may either involve tuning
cross-population connection weights (Arkhipov et al., 2018; Billeh et al., 2020; Dura-Bernal et
al., 2023) or focus on population-specific background inputs (Jiang et al., 2024; Isbister et al.,
2026). Weight tuning favors the model’s biological self-sufficiency but requires jointly
optimizing a very large number of parameters and risks distorting the circuit’s dynamical
properties. By contrast, background-input tuning involves a much smaller parameter set and
keeps connectivity fixed, but makes the baseline activity dependent on an abstract external drive
aggregating multiple unspecified influences. In this work, we follow the background-input
approach and develop a framework intended to make this form of tuning more tractable.

Despite its smaller parameter set, background-input tuning remains a multidimensional
problem. Changing the input to one population affects the whole circuit through recurrent
connectivity, so population-specific inputs have to be tuned jointly. Independent population-
wise tuning becomes possible in the open-loop setup created by circuit decoupling. By itself,
however, this decoupling omits the recurrent inputs that cells normally receive from each other.
Background inputs tuned to produce certain firing rates in the decoupled setup may therefore
yield different rates in the recurrently connected model. Consequently, background-input
tuning methods based on decoupling must account for this potential difference. Our framework
and a method proposed by Isbister et al. (2026) represent two possible ways to address this
mismatch.

Isbister et al. decoupled the circuit without compensating for the omitted recurrent inputs and
addressed the potential rate mismatch in a subsequent tuning stage that involved simulations of
the recurrently connected model. First, in the open-loop setup, they measured how each
population’s firing rate depended on its background input. Next, they estimated a mapping
between population-rate vectors in the open-loop and recurrent setups. To avoid high-
dimensional sampling, they restricted the space of recurrent-model firing rate vectors to a one-
dimensional manifold formed by scalar multiples of the target rate vector they sought to achieve.
The corresponding open-loop rate vectors remained unknown, so the mapping was refined
iteratively: after each round of recurrent-model simulations, they used the resulting rates to

===== PDF PAGE 14 =====
choose candidate background inputs for the next round. In their model, the target rates were
reached after a few iterations, although the study did not establish general conditions for
convergence.

In contrast to Isbister et al., we use a modified open-loop setup where the omitted recurrent
inputs are compensated by surrogate inputs delivering spike trains at the target firing rates of
the corresponding presynaptic populations. These spike trains are generated independently of the
ongoing circuit activity, so our compensation does not introduce coupling and preserves the
open-loop setup. Together, the surrogate inputs to a cell approximate the joint influence it would
experience in the recurrent circuit operating near the target regime.

When the surrogate compensation is sufficiently accurate, the background inputs tuned in the
open-loop setup can yield a near-target regime after recurrent connectivity is restored – as we
found for the cortical part of our model (Fig. FIG_CMP_EE_SURR_FADE(C), red bars). When
substantial errors remain, the open-loop background solution can instead provide the basis for a
second tuning stage involving multidimensional search – as we demonstrated in the thalamic
part of the model (Fig. FIG_THAL(D), red bars). We do not prescribe a specific algorithm, and
various methods could be used for this stage. In principle, our framework is also compatible
with the method of Isbister et al.: compensated open-loop tuning applied to several multiples
of the target state could meaningfully initialize their iterative procedure, potentially improving
convergence. Overall, our method could either yield a directly usable background solution or
help initialize a secondary tuning procedure.

In addition to full decoupling described above, our framework supports partial decoupling that
isolates selected subcircuits from the rest of the network while preserving their internal
connectivity. As before, the full-network environment can be approximated by surrogate
compensation of the omitted inputs. Working with subcircuits helps identify and address tuning
problems in a smaller setting before assembling the full network. It can also reduce the number
of jointly optimized parameters when multidimensional search is needed. A related strategy
was used by Billeh et al. and Dura-Bernal et al. for synaptic weight tuning: they started tuning
from individual layers and progressed toward the full network. In our model, we used
compensated partial decoupling to tune the cortical and thalamic subcircuits separately and
overcame distinct tuning challenges that emerged in each. Overall, the two forms of decoupling
played complementary roles: full decoupling enabled population-wise background tuning, while
partial decoupling provided an intermediate step before full-network reassembly.

Conditions for accurate surrogate compensation
Background inputs tuned in the open-loop setup are intended to yield a near-target regime after
recurrent connectivity is restored. The success of this transfer depends on how closely the
surrogate inputs match the recurrent synaptic inputs that emerge near the target regime. In
our implementation, all units within a surrogate population generated independent Poisson
spike trains at the same rate. This construction simplifies two aspects of recurrent activity:
spike-train correlations and within-population heterogeneity of firing rates. The accuracy of
surrogate compensation therefore depends on how strongly these simplifications alter the inputs
received by postsynaptic cells.

Replacing each presynaptic cell by an independent Poisson generator removes both temporal
correlations within individual spike trains and correlations between cells. This simplification is
broadly consistent with experiments showing irregular spiking in awake cortex and weak
pairwise correlations in both cortex and thalamus (Compte et al., 2003; Maimon and Assad,

===== PDF PAGE 15 =====
2009; Ecker et al., 2010; Erisken et al., 2014; Maksimov et al., 2018). Such activity is well-
studied theoretically and commonly emerges in large recurrent networks through excitation-
inhibition balance and inhibition-driven decorrelation (van Vreeswijk, Sompolinsky, 1998;
Brunel, 2000; Renart et al., 2010; Sanzeni et al., 2022). Our recurrent model produced
irregular spiking and weak correlations as well (suppl.), suggesting the plausibility of the          Добавлено примечание ([НН3]): synonym needed
independent Poisson approximation for the chosen baseline regime.

The other simplification concerns cell-to-cell firing-rate heterogeneity, which is absent from our
surrogate populations whose units all fire at the same rate. By comparison, experiments show
that spontaneous firing rates vary widely across cells in the cortex (Hromádka et al., 2008;
O'Connor et al., 2010) and thalamus (Anderson and Linden, 2011; Bartlett and Wang, 2011).
Broad rate distributions also emerge naturally in network models with random or
heterogeneous connectivity (van Vreeswijk, Sompolinsky, 1998; Roxin et al., 2011; Vegué,
Roxin, 2019; Sanzeni et al., 2022). Our model produced broad within-population rate
distributions as well (suppl.). Thus, equal-rate surrogates do not reproduce this aspect of
recurrent activity, which may reduce the accuracy of surrogate-based tuning.

The negative impact of presynaptic rate averaging depends on the resulting distortion of the
effective input to postsynaptic cells. This distortion is expected to remain small under two
main conditions. First, the mean postsynaptic effect must depend approximately linearly on
presynaptic firing rate, while strong synaptic non-linearities could violate this condition
(Mongillo et al., 2012). Second, each postsynaptic cell must receive input from a sufficiently
large and unbiased sample of presynaptic cells, so that the mean firing rate in that sample stays
close to the presynaptic population mean (Vegué and Roxin, 2019). Violations of either
condition could reduce the accuracy of surrogate compensation and decrease tuning quality.

Testing the open-loop solution in recurrent subcircuits
Imperfect surrogate compensation is not the only reason why recurrent activity may deviate from
the target state. A recurrently connected circuit may have multiple stable regimes and settle
into one that is not closest to the target. We therefore distinguish two problems: inaccuracy
of surrogate compensation and inaccessibility of the desired regime. The two problems are
independent and can coexist, so both must be considered when testing the open-loop solution in
recurrent subcircuits.

The isolated cortical subcircuit provided an example of the accessibility problem. With all
intracortical connections recurrent, the activity settled into a regime far from the target state. We
therefore tested whether the circuit could sustain another regime closer to the target. First, we
found that replacing E→E connections with surrogate inputs was enough to achieve a near-target
regime. Then, starting from this regime, we gradually faded out the surrogate E→E inputs and
restored the recurrent E→E connections. After this transition, the activity remained close to the
target, with population-wise mean errors across model realizations within ±2 Hz (Fig.
FIG_CMP_EE_SURR_FADE(C), red bars). Thus, the same cortical subcircuit was able to
sustain two different regimes, but the one closer to the target was inaccessible with naïve
model initialization. The gradual surrogate-to-recurrent E→E transition provided a way to
reach this regime.

In the thalamic subcircuit, similar tests provided no evidence for an accessibility problem:
replacing different subsets of connections by surrogates improved the result to varying degrees,
but the errors always returned after fading back to recurrent connectivity (Supplementary Fig.
XXX). We therefore interpret these errors primarily as a result of inaccurate surrogate

===== PDF PAGE 16 =====
compensation. Such inaccuracy is consistent with limited presynaptic sampling: cells in the
thalamic subcircuit received fewer recurrent inputs than in the cortex, and the presynaptic
firing rates had broader distributions (Supplementary Fig. XXX). Together, these factors
caused the mean presynaptic firing rate to vary more across postsynaptic cells in the thalamus
(Supplementary Fig. XXX). Consistent with this variability, several thalamic populations
showed substantially larger errors compared to those remaining in the cortex after E→E
fading. In the TC population, for example, the mean error across model realizations was about 10
Hz (Fig. FIG_THAL(D), orange bars). These residual errors were large enough to motivate the
secondary tuning stage.

When surrogate compensation leaves substantial residual errors, the open-loop solution may
still provide a useful starting point for secondary multidimensional tuning. Since the open-
loop solution is approximately valid, the search can focus on small corrections to it – this process
may converge easier than tuning the background input from scratch in the coupled model. We
demonstrated this approach in the isolated thalamic subcircuit with surrogate cortical inputs.          Добавлено примечание ([НН4]): Can be read like

corrections, one per thalamic population. We repeated the tuning for 15 random model

correction reduced the mean error across realizations to within ±5 Hz for every thalamic

Secondary tuning reintroduces multidimensional search, thus reducing the main computational
advantage of full decoupling. Partial decoupling nevertheless simplifies the problem by
restricting the search to individual subcircuits. In our case, isolating the thalamus limited the
coupled search to seven variables. When the thalamic corrections were applied to the full 43-

consistent with restoring thalamocortical and corticothalamic connections that remained

tuning dimensionality, although the accuracy may decrease when the full recurrent connectivity
is restored.

Toolbox and future extensions
We implemented our method as a publicly available toolbox that automatically modifies
NetPyNE-compatible model descriptions. The toolbox applies circuit decoupling with surrogate
compensation based on user-defined rules that specify which connections should be replaced by
surrogate inputs and which populations should be omitted. The toolbox supports full decoupling
for open-loop background tuning, compensated isolation of selected recurrent subcircuits, and
selective connection replacement for pathway freezing or gradual surrogate-to-recurrent fading.
We demonstrated these capabilities in one model and for one target regime, but the toolbox
could be potentially applied to a broad class of NetPyNE-compatible circuit models.

Future extensions could improve the match between surrogate inputs and recurrent activity.
One approach would be to iteratively refine the surrogate inputs to better reproduce the
firing-rate distributions and spike-train statistics observed in the recurrent network. After
each recurrent-network simulation, these activity properties could be measured and used to

===== PDF PAGE 17 =====
construct updated surrogates for the next round of open-loop tuning, with the process repeated
until convergence. Although this procedure would still ignore cross-cell correlations, it could
further reduce errors caused by surrogate-recurrent mismatch.

A promising future direction is to extend the present surrogate-compensated decoupling
framework from stationary firing-rate targets to time-dependent activity, such as evoked
responses or oscillations. For such applications, surrogate inputs would need to capture the
relevant temporal structure in addition to mean firing rates. Although we did not test this
extension here, it could broaden the use of circuit decoupling from establishing baseline regimes
to tuning and analyzing dynamic properties of biophysically detailed network models.

Model
Overview

We used a modified version of the macaque auditory thalamocortical model (A1) developed by
Dura-Bernal et al. (2023). The model was implemented in NetPyNE/NEURON. Most of the
parameters remained unchanged compared to the original version. Below, we provide a brief
overview of the model an describe the changes we made. For details, see Dura-Bernal et al.
(2023) and github.

The simulations analyzed here concerned spontaneous baseline activity.

Cells, populations

The model contained 12,908 neurons in 43 populations, including 36 cortical populations across
layers 1-6 (including L5A/L5B subdivision) and 7 populations in the auditory thalamus and
thalamic reticular nucleus. Cortical excitatory cells belong to the types: L2-6 intratelencphalic
pyramidal (IT), L4 stellate (ITS4), L5-6 corticothalamic (CT), pyramidal-tract-like cells of L5B
(PT5B). Inhibitory cell types: parvalbumin-positive (PV), somatostatin-positive (SOM),
vasoactive-intestinal-peptide-positive (VIP), neurogliaform (NGF). L1 contained only NGF
cells, L2-6 contained all inhibitory types. Thalamus contained MGB thalamocortical cells (TC,
HTC, TCM), MGB interneurons (TI, TIM), and reticular nucleus cells (IRE, IREM). Thalamus
was divided by the core (TC, HTC, TI, IRE) and matrix (TCM, TIM, IREM). Cells were
randomly positioned withing their respective layers based on experimentally estimated densities.

Cell models contained 1-6 compartments (depending on the type) whose membrane properties
were defined by several types of ion channels with biologically realistic parameters. Some of the
cellular parameters were changed compared to the published model, summarized in Table
TBL_PARAMS_NEW. These changes were made for (1) thalamocortical cells to achieve more
realistic single-cell activity and (2) excitatory cortical cells to avoid cell-level multistability.

Connectivity

The circuit had random sparse connectivity with the same connection probabilities and synaptic
delays as in Dura-Bernal et al. (2023). Most of synaptic properties remained the same, except for
revised time constants and reversal potential of GABAB receptors; also, some of the pathways

===== PDF PAGE 18 =====
had a different GABAA:GABAB ratio in our version (see Table TBL_PARAMS_NEW).
Furthermore, we revised the synaptic positioning rules that define pathway-specific lists of
postsynaptic cell sections to choose from. As in the published model, every section contained
one numerical segment, so section choice fully specified synapse location at the resolution of the
cell models. The complete section map is given in Table TBL_SEC.

The main changes involved synaptic weights. Dura-Bernal et al. (2023) combined the available
experimental evidence to build the “original” weight matrix Wdata. Then they introduced a set of
multipliers jointly applied to this matrix and tuned them to fit the required circuit activity. In this
paper, we tuned the background inputs rather than weights, so we removed the multipliers tuned
by Dura-Bernal et al. (2023). Instead, we applied two non-specific multipliers: 0.125 to the
connections between excitatory cells (E-E) and 0.25 to the other connections. The weights of E-
E connections were reduced compared to the others to prevent abnormally synchronized circuit
activity.

Background inputs

Every neuron in our model received a background input comprised of two independently
generated Poisson spike trains delivered to the soma. One spike train provided excitatory input
through an AMPA synapse, and the other – inhibitory input through a GABAA synapse.
Neurons in the same population shared the two input rates and synaptic weights, but received
independently generated spike trains. The synaptic weights were chosen separately for each
population so that an individual background spike (from either excitatory or inhibitory input)
received in the resting state (with all other synaptic inputs turned off) produced a somatic voltage
deflection with the absolute amplitude of approximately 0.5 mV (see wxe and wxi columns in
Table TBL_RX_WX). We chose this value arbitrarily from a biologically plausible EPSP/IPSP
range and used it for each cell for simplicity. Such choice is reasonable due to the abstract nature
of the background inputs that imitate the joint influence of processes not directly accounted for
in the model. The background weights were kept constant, while the background rates (rxe, rxi)
were the tunable variables. All the background inputs that were used in the published version of
the model to provide minimal baseline activity were removed.

For the thalamic and excitatory cortical populations, we also added a tonic offset current to the
soma intended to achieve biologically plausible resting state (Table TBL_IBKG_ADHOC).

When the modeled and surrogate presynaptic populations fired at the same mean rate, the
matched connection rules and scaling factors 𝑘+ (1 −𝑘) = 1 preserved the expected mean

spike trains. Preserving the mean did not impose the same constraint on input variance. For

two equally sized synapse sets with half the original weights produce the same mean input but
half the variance of one full-weight set.

Assumptions

When we estimate the required background input H by using surrogate open-loop models, we
assume that the activity of the surrogate pre-synaptic populations is similar to the activity of the
real populations in a recurrently connected full model. In practice, this similarity is limited.

Surrogate neurons generate independent Poisson spike trains with equal firing rates within a
single population. However, the activity in a full model may differ from it in several ways:

     -   Real neurons may generate non-Poisson spike trains (with CV not equal to 1);
     -   In a connected network, spike trains are correlated between cells;
     -   Recurrent connectivity and complex cellular mechanisms may cause temporal
        correlations of spiking activity (e.g. oscillations);
     -   Cells within a population receive different numbers of inputs, so their firing rates are not
       equal.

When we plug the input H=Hs found in the surrogate open-loop setup into the full model, the
disparities between real and surrogate inputs will make the actual baseline rates R* deviate from
the target rates R0. However, we cannot estimate the magnitude of this deviation in advance. Our
rationale is that R* will be close enough to R0 so it can be used as a good starting point for the
next tuning step that will sufficiently reduce the deviation.

Selecting the background rates

We linearly interpolated each fine firing-rate map 𝑅𝑝 onto a 200 × 200 grid in the grid-index

interpolation-grid cells per axis for cortical populations and approximately 11.7 cells per axis for
thalamic populations.

From the smoothed map, we extracted the contour at the target firing rate 𝑟𝑝0 and selected its
middle point by index (Fig. FIG_BKG_GRID(C)). We converted the (𝑚𝑒, 𝑚𝑖) grid coordinates

this process are given in Table TBL_RX_WX.

Secondary tuning stage

For the second tuning stage, we chose a feedback procedure to jointly determine one constant somatic
current correction per thalamic population. During adaptation, feedback dynamically adjusted the currents
to bring population firing rates toward their targets. We allowed the controlled activity to settle, then
disabled feedback and locked the learned tonic corrections. To obtain corrections that could maintain
firing rates near their targets after feedback ended, we accumulated a slow correction from the slowly
varying firing-rate error and used an additional transient correction during adaptation. At locking, we
retained the slow correction and removed the transient one.

Population firing-rate estimate

For population 𝑃 containing 𝑁𝑝 neurons, we defined the normalized population spike train as

                                         𝑁𝑝
                              1
                            𝑥𝑝(𝑡) = 𝑁𝑝∑∑𝛿(𝑡−𝑡𝑝𝑖𝑘),
                                                𝑘
                                             𝑖=1

where 𝑡𝑝𝑖𝑘 is the time at which spike 𝑘 from neuron 𝑖 reached the controller, including a delivery delay
of 1 𝑚𝑠. We estimated the running mean firing rate 𝑟𝑝(𝑡) by exponential filtering with time constant
𝜏𝑟= 100 𝑚𝑠:

                                 𝜏𝑟𝑟̇𝑝= −𝑟𝑝+ 𝑐𝑡𝑥𝑝(𝑡).

We used the conversion factor 𝑐𝑡= 103 𝑚𝑠 𝑠−1 to express the rate estimate in Herz while 𝑡 is given in
milliseconds. Each delivered spike increased 𝑟𝑝 by 𝑐𝑡/(𝑁𝑝𝜏𝑟), and between spikes 𝑟𝑝 decayed
exponentially with time constant 𝜏𝑟. Normalization by 𝑁𝑝 yielded the mean firing rate per neuron.

Feedback control of tonic current

We defined the firing-rate error as 𝑒𝑝(𝑡) = 𝑟𝑝0 −𝑟𝑝(𝑡), where 𝑟𝑝0 is the target rate of population 𝑃. We
filtered this error to obtain its slowly varying component, 𝑚𝑝, and used the residual 𝑒𝑝−𝑚𝑝 to calculate
the transient correction. During adaptation, the controller states evolved according to:

                          𝜏𝑚𝑚̇𝑝     = 𝑒𝑝−𝑚𝑝,
                                 𝜏𝑢𝑢̇𝑝  = −𝑢𝑝+ 𝑘𝑢(𝑒𝑝−𝑚𝑝),
                                               𝑠̇𝑝     = 𝑘𝑠𝜙𝜀(𝑚𝑝),
                              𝑧𝑝      = 𝑠𝑝+ 𝑢𝑝.

Here, 𝜏𝑚 is the time constant for filtering the error, 𝑢𝑝 is the transient correction, and 𝜏𝑢 and 𝑘𝑢 are its
filtering time constant and gain. The state 𝑠𝑝 is the accumulated slow correction, 𝑘𝑠 is its integrator gain,
and 𝑧𝑝 is the total controller output.

===== PDF PAGE 24 =====
The slow correction 𝑠𝑝 responded to the slow error component 𝑚𝑝, while the transient correction 𝑢𝑝
responded to the residual 𝑒𝑝−𝑚𝑝. The transient-correction gain 𝑘𝑢 was small, limiting the contribution
of 𝑢𝑝 during adaptation. This separation allowed the slow branch 𝑠𝑝 to accumulate the correction for a
persistent firing-rate mismatch without relying on a fast dynamic feedback created by 𝑢𝑝.

Even near the target, firing rates fluctuate due to stochastic spiking. To make 𝑠𝑝 less sensible to such
fluctuations, we introduced a dead zone:

                            𝑚𝑝−𝜀,  𝑚𝑝> 𝜀,
                        𝜙𝜀(𝑚𝑝) = {   0,     |𝑚𝑝| ≤𝜀,
                       𝑚𝑝+ 𝜀, 𝑚𝑝< −𝜀.

The slow correction 𝑠𝑝 therefore accumulated only the portion of the filtered error that exceeded the dead
zone.

The controller parameter values are given in Table TBL_CTRL_PARAMS.

To close the feedback loop, we used the controller output 𝑧𝑝 to drive a somatic current injected to every
cell of the controlled population 𝑃:

                                       𝐼𝑝(𝑡) = 𝑔𝜇𝑧𝑝(𝑡).

The gain 𝑔𝜇 was chosen to balance the convergence time and stability of the system.

When the population activity 𝑟𝑝(𝑡) was below the target rate 𝑟𝑝0, the error 𝑒𝑝(𝑡) = 𝑟𝑝0 −𝑟𝑝(𝑡) was
potitive, as well as its filtered version 𝑚𝑝. With 𝑚𝑝> 𝜀, the feedback current 𝐼𝑝 changed in the
depolarizing direction, tending to increase 𝑟𝑝(𝑡). Conversely, when 𝑟𝑝(𝑡) was above 𝑟𝑝0, the feedback
current changed towards hyperpolarization. Thus, the feedback controller worked to keep the activity
close to the target 𝑟𝑝0.

We implemented the controller equations in a NEURON mechanism that received every output spike
from the controlled population 𝑃 with the weight 1⁄ 𝑁𝑝 . The mechanism's output drove a current
delivered to the soma of every neuron in 𝑃. In distributed simulations, each MPI rank had its own
instance of the controller mechanism. Each instance received spikes from all neurons in 𝑃, including
those on other ranks, and supplied the same control signal to the neurons located on its own rank.

Tuning protocol and static corrections

To determine the current corrections, we ran simulations lasting 220 𝑠. We initialized the rate estimate
and controller states as 𝑟𝑝= 𝑚𝑝= 𝑢𝑝= 0 and 𝑠𝑝= 𝑧0 = 0 𝐻𝑧, where 𝑧0 is the initial controller
output. During the first 10 𝑠, only the rate estimate evolved, and the controller output remained at 𝑧0.
Adaptation began at 𝑡0 = 10 𝑠 and continued until 𝑡𝑙𝑜𝑐𝑘= 160 𝑠. At locking, we froze the controller
states and removed the transient correction, leaving the learned slow correction as the tonic output:

                                               𝑧0,       𝑡< 𝑡0,
                         𝑧𝑝(𝑡) = {𝑠𝑝(𝑡) + 𝑢𝑝(𝑡),   𝑡0 ≤𝑡≤𝑡𝑙𝑜𝑐𝑘,
                                          𝑠𝑝(𝑡𝑙𝑜𝑐𝑘),    𝑡> 𝑡𝑙𝑜𝑐𝑘.

We calculated mean population firing rates over 200–220 𝑠 to assess the result.

We repeated the tuning procedure across 15 independent realizations of the model. For realization 𝑗, the
fixed current correction for population 𝑃 was:

                                        𝐼𝑝(𝑗) = 𝑔𝜇𝑠𝑝(𝑗)(𝑡𝑙𝑜𝑐𝑘).

===== PDF PAGE 25 =====
We obtained one static correction per population by taking the median over model realizations:

                              𝐼̃𝑝= median{𝐼𝑝(1), … , 𝐼𝑝(15)}.

Taking the median limited the influence of extreme current estimates. The resulting static corrections are
given in Table TBL_STATIC_CURRENT_CORRECTIONS. (Figure link here).

In subsequent simulations, every neuron in population 𝑃 received the additional constant somatic current
𝐼̃𝑝 without active feedback control.

Methods (old)

Model
We used a modified version of our previously published biophysical model of thalamocortical
A1 column.

In the old model, we initialized the synaptic weights based on experimental data, and then scaled
them by population-specific multipliers found during optimization. In the present study, we took
the original weights from the old model (without multipliers) and kept them constant along the
tuning process.

Cellular and synaptic properties, as well as connection probabilities, were taken from the old
model unmodified or with minor changes.

Background spiking inputs in the old model were excitatory, and their parameters were chosen in
a way to provide minimal activity levels in isolated neurons. In the present model, we imitate the
input from brain regions outside the modeled column by delivering two Poisson spike trains to
the soma of every neuron – via AMPA and GABAA synapses, respectively. The synaptic
weights wxe, wxi were chosen to provide the EPSP/IPSP amplitudes of 0.5 mV, and the
background firing rates rxe, rxi underwent an optimization process. All neurons within a single
population received the background inputs with the same properties, and each Poisson spike train
was sampled independently.

Surrogate open-loop circuits
Direct optimization of background inputs in a multi-population model is computationally
prohibitive due to the high dimensionality of the parameter space. To address this, we developed
a method that transforms a recurrent network into a set of decoupled open-loop surrogate
models, allowing the background parameters to be tuned independently for each population. This
reduces a single high-dimensional optimization problem to many low-dimensional ones, which
can be solved efficiently and in parallel.

The method is illustrated in Figure FIG_SURR_NET for a circuit comprising of two populations
P1 and P2. Figure FIG_SURR_NET(A) shows the original circuit with recurrent connections
Cnm (m,n=1,2), where each Cnm represents the full set of connections originating from Pm cells
and targeting Pn cells. Also, each cell of Pn receives a background input from two Poisson spike

===== PDF PAGE 34 =====
generators with the rates rxen, rrin and the weights wxen, wxin, connected via AMPA and
GABAA synapses, respectively.

The corresponding surrogate circuit is shown in Figure FIG_SURR_NET(B). For each
population Pn (n=1,2) in the original circuit, we build a surrogate population Pn’, consisting of
independent Poisson spike generators firing at the rate rn0 that is the target rate we want to
achieve for Pn. Pseudo-cells of Pn’ are spatially distributed according to the same rules as the
cells of Pn, i.e. they occupy the same spatial region with the same density.

For each pair of populations Pm, Pn (m,n=1,2), we remove Pm→ Pn connections (i.e.
connections from cells of Pm to cells of Pn) and replace them by equivalent Pm′→ Pn
connections originating from spike-generating pseudo-cells of Pm’. We create these new
connections according to the same rules that were used for Pm→ Pn in the original model (Cnm
in panels A and B). Thus, connection probabilities remain the same, and the distributions of
synaptic weights and transmission delays are preserved. Distance-dependent terms in the
connectivity rules are automatically reproduced as Pm’ and Pm have the same spatial
distribution. Background inputs in the surrogate model are kept unchanged.

Thus, in the surrogate model (panel B), population P1 has four inputs: (1) background spike
generators (rxe1, wxe1) connected to P1 cells in a 1-to-1 manner via AMPA synapses, (2)
background generators (rxi1, wxi1) connected 1-to-1 via GABAA synapses, (3) spike-generating
pseudo-cells of P1’, firing at the rate r10 (target rate for P1 we want to achieve) and connected to
P1 cells according to the set of rules C11, (4) pseudo-cells of P2’ with the rate r20, connected
according to the rules C21. Similarly, P2 receives background inputs with the rates rxe2 and
rxi2, as well as surrogate inputs from P1’ and P2’ with the rates r10 and r20, respectively.

In the original model (panel A), cells receive the same background inputs as in the surrogate
model (panel B), but instead of the surrogate inputs from P1’ and P2’ with the rates r10 and r20,
they receive recurrent inputs from P1 and P2 with some average rates r1 and r2. Our main idea
is: if we tune the background input rates rx=(rxe1, rxi1, rxe2, rxi2) in such way that P1 and P2 in
the surrogate model fire at the rates r1=r10, r2=r20, and then plug this rx into the original model,
we might expect that it will also settle into the state (r10, r20). Indeed, the original model is
equivalent to the surrogate one in this case: inputs received by a cell, in average, have the same
rates and weights in both models, and the mean number of inputs is also the same. Existence and
stability of the state (r10, r20) with recurrent connectivity is discussed below in the paper.

Background input tuning
With the surrogate setup, we were able to tune the background inputs for each population
separately. All neurons that belong to the same population receive background inputs with the
same properties, so for each population P, we need to find a pair of firing rates: hp =(rxpe, rxpi),
while the weights (wxe, wxi ) are fixed to the values that yield |PSP|=0.5 mV. Thus, for the full
A1 model with N=43 populations, our task is to find a 2N-dimensional background input vector:
H=(h1,…,hN) that sets the model to the target baseline state R0 given by a vector of target firing
rates for individual populations: R0=(r10,…,rN0).

Following the logic described above, we replace this 2N-dimensional task by N 2-dimensional
tasks and search for the input H that produces the target state R0 in an open-loop surrogate
model. Fig. FIG_BKG_GRID(A) shows the surrogate setup for a population P. The node A1’

===== PDF PAGE 36 =====
represents a surrogate copy of the whole A1 circuit, containing all 43 surrogate populations
P1’,…,PN’ emitting Poisson spikes at the corresponding target rates r10,…,rN0. The link
A1’->P encapsulates all the synaptic inputs of P that were recurrent in A1 and now are rewired to
originate from surrogate spike generators (with the same connectivity properties). The upper link
represents the background inputs of P, whose weights wxpe, wxpi are fixed (tuned for |PSP|=0.5
mV), and the rates rxpe, rxpi undergo a grid search process described below. This process and its
results are illustrated in Fig. FIG_BKG_GRID(B-E), using P=IT5B population as an example.

More precisely, for each population P, we go through the following steps:

-  Build a surrogate model, in which all recurrent inputs of P are replaced by equivalent spike
   generators (panel A);
-  Choose a region for broad parameter scanning as a rectangle on the (rxpe, rxpi) parameter
   plane, with a reasonable range of parameters (e.g. for IT5B we used rxe=0..25 kHz, rxi=0..5
   kHz);
-  Perform broad parameter scanning: run simulations with (rxpe, rxpi) located at the nodes
   of a grid covering the chosen rectangular region. For each simulation, compute the mean
   firing rate of population P, thus producing a 2-D rate matrix Rpb (panel B, heatmap).
-  Choose a region Qp for fine parameter scanning. We define Qp as a parallelogram on the
   (rxpe, rxpi) parameter plane, where the firing rate of P is close to the target value – i.e. a
   narrow strip around Rpb=rp0 contour. Panel B shows the region Qp in (rxpe, rxpi)
   coordinates, and panel C shows Qp in transformed coordinates, where the parallelogram turns
   into a rectangle.
-  Scan the parameters over Qp and build the firing rate matrix Rp. Run simulations with
   (rxpe, rxpi) located at the nodes of a grid covering Qp. Build a 2-D matrix Rp containing the
   average firing rates of P measured for the grid nodes (panel C, heatmap).
-  Find 1-D contour where the target rate is reached: Rp=rp0 (black curve on panel C).
-  Choose a point hp*=(rxpe*, rxpi*) on the contour (red dot on panel C). We chose hp* as
   the midpoint of the contour, but one could also use a second biophysical constraint such as
  CV or mean voltage.
Panel D shows the firing rate dynamics of population P in the surrogate setup (panel A) under
the chosen background input hp*=(rxpe*, rxpe*). The rp(t) trace confirms the validity of the hp*
choice: after an initial transient period, the firing rate of P fluctuates around the target value rp0.
Panel E shows the statistics of the average firing rate Rp=<rp(t)> across multiple random seeds
that govern the sampling of connectivity patterns and Poisson spike trains. One can see that the
results are robust, and Rp remains close to the target rate rp0 for various seeds.
Although the procedure above is written for a single population P, in practice all populations
were simulated together. For each population, we defined its own search region Qp on the (rxe,
rxi) plane, with the same grid resolution across populations. Thus, each simulation corresponded
to a single grid-node index shared across populations, while the actual (rxpi, rxpe) values were
determined for each population individually by its own region Qp. Surrogate presynaptic spike
generators were shared across the populations. Simulations were parallelized across the grid
nodes. The same workflow was used for the initial coarse parameter scanning over rectangular
(rxe, rxi) regions, which were also chosen for each population separately.

Partially surrogate models
In addition to the fully surrogate setup described above, our framework allows construction of
partially surrogate models, in which only selected connections are rewired to originate from
surrogate populations, while the remaining connections stay recurrent, as in the full model. Thus,
for a given postsynaptic population, inputs from some presynaptic populations can be replaced
by their surrogate copies, whereas other inputs can remain driven by real neurons. If all inputs
and outputs of a population are surrogate, that population no longer needs to be explicitly
simulated and can be removed from the network.

First, one can keep most of the recurrent network intact while replacing selected connections
with surrogate inputs. Comparing such variants with the full model helps reveal the dynamical
role of specific connections. In particular, this approach can identify connections that destabilize
network activity or, conversely, contribute to its stabilization.

Second, one can isolate a subcircuit of interest by keeping only a selected set of populations and
their mutual recurrent connections intact, while replacing the rest of the network with surrogate

===== PDF PAGE 38 =====
inputs. This enables analysis of individual circuit components within a realistic network context
at substantially reduced computational cost. For example, a single cortical layer can be studied
while the remainder of the circuit is represented by surrogate populations.


Semi-surrogate connections
We also implemented semi-surrogate connections, in which an input pathway is split between
recurrent and surrogate components. For each postsynaptic neuron, the original set of synapses is
duplicated: one half receives recurrent inputs from real neurons, while the other half receives
surrogate inputs from spike generators.
Synaptic weights are proportionally divided between the two components. If the original
synaptic weight is w, then recurrent synapses have the weight kw, while surrogate synapses have
the weight (1−k)w, where k∈[0,1] is a splitting coefficient. The limiting cases k=0 and k=1
correspond to fully surrogate and fully recurrent inputs, respectively.
Since the recurrent and surrogate weights always sum to w, the mean total input remains the
same for any k. However, this equivalence does not extend to higher-order statistics. For
example, N synapses with weight w generally yield larger input variance than 2N synapses with
weight w/2.
We also implemented a time-dependent version of the splitting coefficient k(t), allowing smooth
transitions between surrogate and recurrent connectivity setups.

Assumptions
When we estimate the required background input H by using surrogate open-loop models, we
assume that the activity of the surrogate pre-synaptic populations is similar to the activity of the
real populations in a recurrently connected full model. In practice, this similarity is limited.

Surrogate neurons generate independent Poisson spike trains with equal firing rates within a
single population. However, the activity in a full model may differ from it in several ways:

     -   Real neurons may generate non-Poisson spike trains (with CV not equal to 1);
     -   In a connected network, spike trains are correlated between cells;
     -   Recurrent connectivity and complex cellular mechanisms may cause temporal
        correlations of spiking activity (e.g. oscillations);
     -   Cells within a population receive different numbers of inputs, so their firing rates are not
       equal.

When we plug the input H=Hs found in the surrogate open-loop setup into the full model, the
disparities between real and surrogate inputs will make the actual baseline rates R* deviate from
the target rates R0. However, we cannot estimate the magnitude of this deviation in advance. Our
rationale is that R* will be close enough to R0 so it can be used as a good starting point for the
next tuning step that will sufficiently reduce the deviation.

Existence of the target steady state
It is not guaranteed that there exists a background input H, under which the original model has a
steady state matching the chosen target R0. First, it is possible that R0 is not a fixed point for any
input H (e.g. if Rn is larger than the maximum firing rate achievable by the neurons of the n-th
population). Second, even if the fixed point R0 exists for some input H, it could be unstable.

===== PDF PAGE 39 =====
The existence of H that yields the steady state R0 cannot be assessed in advance. We determine
the input Hs that yields the target state R0 in the surrogate open-loop setup, and run an
optimization process in the full model starting from Hs. We consider the outcome to be
successful if the resulting firing rate dynamics remains sufficiently close to R0. Technically, it
may correspond either to a steady state (close to) R0 or to an orbit swirling around R0.

With our original selection of parameters (based on our previously published A1 model), an
input Hs plugged to the full model yielded unrealistically synchronous activity. Thus, we decided
to decrease the weights of exc-exc connections by 50%.
